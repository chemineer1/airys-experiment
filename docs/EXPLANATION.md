# The argument for a stationary Earth

Malcolm Bowden's video about Airy's experiment argues that filling a telescope with water can tell whether Earth moves past the stars or the stars and their light move around a stationary Earth. It concludes that Airy's result proves Earth is stationary. That conclusion is wrong: its moving-telescope prediction leaves out how moving water changes the light's direction. [1]

The starting point is a real effect. A telescope moving sideways through incoming starlight must tilt toward its motion to keep the star centered: its detector moves while the light travels down the tube. This apparent change in a star's direction is stellar aberration. The video proposes that moving ether carrying stars and starlight past a stationary telescope could produce the same initial tilt. It then claims water can distinguish the two pictures.

### The video's moving-Earth prediction

At 1:45–2:19, the telescope moves sideways and the incoming light falls vertically. Water slows the light, leaving more time for the telescope to move before the photon reaches the bottom. The animation shows a miss at the original tilt and a hit after extra tilt. Its illustrative angles are 5° before filling and 10° afterward.

### The video's stationary-Earth prediction

At 2:24–3:05, the telescope stays still while ether carries starlight past it at an angle. The video keeps that angled path through the water, slows the photon, and shows it reaching the same place. It therefore predicts no extra tilt in this case. At 4:25–4:42, it treats Airy's unchanged result as choosing this picture and proving a stationary Earth.

### Why that conclusion does not follow

The moving-Earth prediction is incorrect. In the star's frame, the water moves with the telescope. Light inside that moving water has a sideways velocity as well as a downward velocity. The video gives the telescope the extra displacement associated with a longer transit but omits the corresponding sideways motion of the light. Once both are included, a moving telescope also needs no extra tilt. The unchanged result therefore cannot make the distinction the video promises.

## What Airy actually measured

George Biddell Airy was Britain's Astronomer Royal. His 1871 report addressed a dispute about how light behaves in moving transparent material. Wilhelm Klinkerfues had predicted—and reported measuring—an increase in stellar aberration when light passed through water inside a telescope. Airy built a more thorough test. [2]

He observed Gamma Draconis at Greenwich with a specially designed water-filled telescope. He compared observations from seasons when ordinary aberration had opposite signs, using astronomical tables that already included that effect. The observations agreed with ordinary aberration; they did not show the proposed increase. Airy rejected Klinkerfues's prediction. He did not report that Earth was stationary. [2]

The word “null” refers to the additional effect sought: no detected increase caused by the water. It does not mean there was no aberration, no required pointing correction, or no motion.

The stationary-Earth interpretation is a later reinterpretation of this result, promoted today as “Airy's failure.” It is not just an invention of recent social media: twentieth-century geocentric writers also advanced it. [1]

### What the video misreads in Airy's report

At 3:32–3:46, the video presents a predicted discrepancy of 30 arcseconds (0.008333°) as what Airy should have found if Earth moved. In the report, that discrepancy follows specifically from Klinkerfues's proposed extra aberration. The table compares instrument latitudes inferred from spring and autumn observations, with ordinary aberration already included in the astronomical tables. Those mean latitudes differ by only 0.8 arcseconds (0.000222°). That rejects the proposed extra effect; it does not reject Earth's motion. [2, p. 38]

At 3:51–4:20, the video interprets a diminishing value of about 100 arcseconds (0.027778°) as evidence that light was slowing down. Airy was describing Gamma Draconis's mean zenith distance at Greenwich: its angular distance from directly overhead. He was not describing the size of stellar aberration or reporting a decrease in light speed. [2, p. 36]

## Ether drag: the historical question

In nineteenth-century wave theories, light was thought to travel through a medium called the ether. A central question was what happened to that ether inside moving matter. Did transparent material carry it along, and if so, by how much?

Fresnel proposed partial ether drag: moving transparent matter would carry along part of the ether associated with it. His theory predicted that adding water to a telescope would not change stellar aberration. An unchanged result was therefore already a prediction of an established optical theory before Airy performed his experiment. [3]

Airy's immediate target was Klinkerfues's competing prediction of an increase, which Klinkerfues claimed followed from wave theory and had observed experimentally. Airy's report explicitly presents the dispute as one about the direction of light in a moving refracting medium. His result rejected that increase and agreed with the unchanged aberration predicted by Fresnel's theory. It did not show that Earth was stationary, nor did it establish that ether existed. [2, 3]

Special relativity later explained light propagation in moving material without requiring a material ether. Laue showed in 1907 that relativistic velocity addition gives Fresnel's drag coefficient as a low-speed approximation. The observed moving-medium effect remains; its explanation no longer requires ether to be physically carried along. [4]

That history matters here: the water's motion was part of the optical question from the beginning. A modern argument that slows the light but ignores the moving medium leaves out the very effect these theories addressed.

## The precise incorrect assumption

The video's error is not that it gives the photon more time in water. That part is right. The error is slowing the photon while keeping its path vertical in the frame where the telescope and water are moving. Its moving-telescope animation therefore leaves out part of the transmitted light's velocity.

When we say light travels at speed 1/n in water, that is the speed measured in the water's rest frame. In that frame, a ray entering straight along the tube stays on its axis and takes longer to reach the stationary detector. To describe this path in the star's frame, both velocity components must be transformed. The resulting ray inside moving water is not vertical.

This is why the video's two drawings appear to disagree. Its stationary-telescope drawing lets the light keep the aligned path measured beside the water. Its moving-telescope drawing slows the light without consistently transforming that path into the other frame. The different predictions come from those inconsistent optical rules, not from a demonstrated difference between absolute rest and motion.

Look first from beside the telescope, moving with it. The telescope, water, and detector are all stationary. Aim the tube along the incoming starlight. In the simplified instrument shown here, the entrance is perpendicular to the tube, so the ray enters the water straight on. Refraction then changes its speed without changing its direction. It travels along the same central axis, only more slowly. Waiting longer for light to reach a stationary detector cannot make a side wall move into its path.

Now describe that same journey from beside the star. The telescope and water move sideways. In these coordinates, the light inside the water also has a sideways velocity. The argument gives the telescope extra sideways displacement during the longer transit but leaves out this change in the light's motion. That omission creates the predicted miss.

This does not mean the light simply takes on the water's full sideways speed. The detector is already offset from the entrance because the telescope is tilted. The correct ray velocity and the moving detector's position must be calculated together. They meet without any extra adjustment.

## What special relativity guarantees

A reference frame is a choice of clocks and coordinates for describing events. “The telescope is at rest” identifies one such choice; it does not assert that Earth is absolutely stationary. Our left panel is a description from beside the telescope, not an endorsement of the video's moving-ether proposal.

Describing the same experiment from beside the star requires transforming the incoming light, the water's motion, the telescope's geometry, and the timing together. Merely swapping which object is called moving while keeping the same light-path assumptions does not do this. Nor does relativity say that physically changing only the source's motion or the illumination must leave an experiment unchanged. The two panels represent corresponding conditions for the same experiment.

Special relativity requires the two frames to agree on a physical encounter. If light strikes the detector's center in one frame, changing coordinates cannot turn that same encounter into a miss. Positions, times, and drawn angles can differ; whether the light touches the detector cannot. Einstein's coordinate and velocity transformations provide the quantitative rule. [4]

We still have to establish what the light actually does. The optical assumptions give the centered path in the telescope's frame; relativity carries that result consistently into the star's frame. The following calculation shows both, including the entire path through the moving tube.

## The relativistic proof

This is an exact proof for the idealized instrument in this simulation. The telescope and a uniform, isotropic, nondispersive medium move together at constant velocity. The incoming ray passes through the entrance center, perpendicular to the entrance surface in the telescope’s rest frame, with misalignment set to zero. The rear detector moves with the tube. Airy's real instrument also had objective lenses and image-forming optics; this model isolates the central-ray question.

Use natural units: vacuum light speed and telescope rest length are both 1. Let β be the signed telescope velocity in the star's frame, and n the refractive index measured in the water's rest frame. Vacuum has n = 1; a filled telescope has n > 1. Define:

$$
c=L=1,\qquad |\beta|<1,\qquad \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}
$$

Primes label telescope coordinates. Unprimed coordinates belong to the star's frame. The ray enters at x = y = t = 0 in both frames. Choose x along the relative motion and y upward, so the incoming vacuum ray travels vertically downward in the star's frame.

### 1. Find the tilt before adding water

In the star's frame the incoming velocity is (0, −1). Transforming it into telescope coordinates gives:

$$
\mathbf{u}'_{\mathrm{in}}=\left(-\beta,-\frac{1}{\gamma}\right),\qquad \sin\alpha=\beta
$$

Its magnitude is 1. The angle α is the apparent star direction measured from the upward reference by the telescope observer. Aim the tube in that direction; its inward unit axis d equals this incoming velocity. The detector center is therefore at position d. This fixes the telescope's alignment before choosing the fill.

### 2. Follow the ray beside the telescope

At normal incidence, the transmitted ray keeps its direction. Its speed becomes 1/n. Its position and the detector-center position are:

$$
\mathbf{r}'_{\mathrm{ray}}(t')=\frac{t'}{n}\,\mathbf{d},\qquad \mathbf{r}'_{D}=\mathbf{d}
$$

At time t' = n, the ray reaches d exactly. Before then, t'/n lies between 0 and 1, so every point of the journey lies on the segment from the entrance center to the detector center. The ray never reaches a side wall. Vacuum traverses that same segment in time 1; water takes time n.

Nothing in the required pointing direction depends on n. Thus:

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{filled}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{vacuum}}=\arcsin\beta,\qquad \Delta\alpha=0
$$

This is the null additional aberration. The original tilt remains; no extra tilt is introduced by the fill.

### 3. Calculate the light's motion beside the star

To test the disputed moving-telescope picture explicitly, transform the transmitted velocity. Einstein's velocity-addition rule in our units is:

$$
u_x=\frac{u'_x+\beta}{1+\beta u'_x},\qquad u_y=\frac{u'_y}{\gamma(1+\beta u'_x)}
$$

Substituting the telescope-frame velocity d/n gives:

$$
u_x=\frac{\beta(n-1)}{n-\beta^2},\qquad u_y=-\frac{1}{\gamma^2(n-\beta^2)}
$$

Here is the missing motion. For moving water, with β ≠ 0 and n > 1, the sideways velocity is nonzero. The ray does not continue vertically at speed 1/n. For n = 1, the formula correctly returns the original vacuum velocity (0, −1). No telescope angle has been changed.

### 4. Meet the moving detector

The apparatus must be described in the same coordinates as the ray. The Lorentz transformation is:

$$
x=\gamma(x'+\beta t'),\qquad y=y',\qquad t=\gamma(t'+\beta x')
$$

For a fixed point of the telescope, eliminating t' gives x = βt + x'/γ. Apply this to the detector center, whose rest coordinates are (−β, −1/γ):

$$
x_D(t)=\beta t-\frac{\beta}{\gamma},\qquad y_D(t)=-\frac{1}{\gamma}
$$

Multiplying each component of the ray's velocity by t gives its position. Equating its y coordinate to the detector's y coordinate gives the candidate arrival time:

$$
T=\gamma(n-\beta^2)>0
$$

At that time, their x coordinates agree too:

$$
x_{\mathrm{ray}}(T)=\gamma\beta(n-1)=x_D(T)
$$

Both y coordinates equal −1/γ. The light and the detector center meet at the same place and time, for every n ≥ 1 and every |β| < 1. Filling changes the arrival event, but the ray still hits the same material point on the detector.

### 5. Check that no wall is hit along the way

An endpoint agreement alone might leave a question about the journey. Let s run from 0 at the entrance to 1 at the detector. At a fixed star-frame time t, the moving tube's central axis is:

$$
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{axis}}(s,t)=\left(\beta t-\frac{s\beta}{\gamma},-\frac{s}{\gamma}\right)
$$

Insert s = t/T and the arrival time T above. Using the calculated light velocity gives:

$$
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{ray}}(t)=\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{axis}}\!\left(\frac{t}{T},t\right),\qquad 0\leq t\leq T
$$

The ray stays on the moving tube's centerline for its entire journey. It cannot hit a side wall. The longer transit and the sideways motion of the light fit together exactly, at the original pointing angle.

## What to look for in the simulation

Set misalignment to zero and change the refractive index. The vacuum and filled rays both reach the center; for n > 1, the filled ray arrives later. The red comparison keeps the transmitted light vertical at speed 1/n in the star's frame. It makes the incorrect assumption discussed above visible. For nonzero relative speed and n > 1, that path misses the center or strikes a wall, depending on tube width. At zero speed or n = 1, this incorrect model happens to agree with the correct path.

Each panel calculates its ray–apparatus intersection from its own starting frame. The calculations use the same physical laws and corresponding illumination, then their contact events are checked by a Lorentz transformation. Making the incident light vertical in both frames would describe different illumination, not this same experiment.

The protractors report the angle measured in the telescope's rest frame. The moving protractor contracts with the tube, including its graduations, so its shape changes while the reading stays the same. Ordinary geometric angles measured on the screen are not generally equal across frames.

Airy's result rules out the claimed extra aberration; it does not select an absolutely stationary Earth. This calculation explains why special relativity is consistent with that result. The experiment alone does not uniquely establish relativity or determine Earth's shape.
